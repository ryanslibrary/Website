Smart Switches, WiFi Dimmers & Smart Home Devices Explained

📅 2026 Guide ⏱ 5 min read 📌 Kent — Sevenoaks, Tunbridge Wells, Tonbridge

Smart plugs, WiFi dimmers, smart switches, hubs — the range of "smart home" kit on the shelf at any DIY store has exploded, and most of it looks like a five-minute job on the box. Some of it genuinely is. Some of it isn't, for a reason that only becomes obvious once you've taken the old switch plate off the wall. Here's what's actually involved.

The easy stuff: no electrician needed

Smart plugs and smart bulbs are the genuine no-installation option. A smart plug sits in an existing socket and switches whatever's plugged into it; a smart bulb screws or bayonets into an existing fitting the same as any other bulb. Both talk to your WiFi or a hub and both are entirely safe to fit yourself — there's no wiring involved, just an app and a few minutes of setup. If you want dimming or scheduling on one lamp or one appliance without touching a wall switch, this is the whole job.

Smart switches and WiFi dimmers — the neutral wire problem

This is where most people hit a wall, sometimes literally. A traditional light switch only ever needed two wires: live in, switched live out. It never needed a neutral, because it was never doing anything except physically breaking the circuit. A smart switch or WiFi dimmer is different — it has its own electronics that need to stay powered even when the light is off, so it can listen for your app or voice command. To do that, most of them need a neutral wire present at the switch itself.

💡 If your existing light switch only has two wires in the back box (live and switched live, no neutral), most WiFi dimmers and smart switches won't work there properly without extra wiring — this is the single biggest reason a smart switch upgrade needs more than just swapping a plate, and exactly the kind of thing worth having assessed before you buy the wrong device for your wiring.

Whether a neutral is present at your switch depends entirely on how and when your house was wired. Many properties, particularly anything wired before neutral-at-switch became a more common (and from the 18th Edition of the wiring regulations onward, increasingly expected) practice, simply never ran one there — there was no need to. Newer builds are more likely to have it, but it's never guaranteed, and it can vary switch by switch in the same house depending on which circuit and which electrician wired that particular room. Working out which switches in your home have one and which don't — and what running a neutral to the rest would actually involve — is exactly the kind of assessment an electrician does in a few minutes on a site visit.

No-neutral smart switches — a compromise, not a fix

Manufacturers make "no-neutral" smart switches for exactly this situation. They work by drawing a very small trickle of current through the light fitting itself to keep their electronics powered, even when the light is notionally off. That workaround comes with real trade-offs: a faint flicker or glow from LED bulbs when they're supposedly switched off, occasional buzzing, and narrower compatibility — they don't play well with every bulb, and some LED drivers react badly to that trickle current over time. An electrician can tell you upfront whether a no-neutral switch will actually behave itself with your specific bulbs, or whether running a proper neutral feed is worth doing once rather than living with a switch that flickers.

Dimmable bulbs — the other common gotcha

A WiFi dimmer only dims a bulb that's actually rated as dimmable. Not all LED bulbs are — a non-dimmable LED on a dimmer circuit will typically flicker, buzz, or simply not dim smoothly, regardless of how good the switch itself is. If you're fitting a dimmer, check the bulbs going with it are explicitly marked dimmable, ideally from the same manufacturer's compatibility list as the switch — mixing brands here is the most common cause of a dimmer that "doesn't work properly" despite being installed correctly.

Hubs, WiFi, Zigbee and Z-Wave

Most smart plugs and bulbs connect straight to your home WiFi. Some ecosystems (many Zigbee and Z-Wave devices, and some premium switch brands) use a separate low-power mesh network instead, which needs its own hub plugged in somewhere central. The upside of a mesh system is generally better reliability and battery life for battery-powered accessories (sensors, some smart blinds); the downside is one more box to manage and, occasionally, one more thing that needs power-cycling when something stops responding. Neither approach is "better" outright — it depends what you're building.

Is it notifiable work under Part P?

A straightforward like-for-like accessory swap — an existing single switch replaced with a smart switch, or an existing dimmer replaced with a WiFi one, in a normal room — generally isn't notifiable work. It becomes notifiable if the job involves anything beyond that: running a new circuit or new wiring (including adding a neutral where there wasn't one), work in a special location such as a bathroom, or any consumer unit changes alongside it. Your electrician will tell you which category a specific job falls into before starting — it's usually a very quick call.

Why it's worth getting an electrician in

Smart plugs and smart bulbs are the one genuine exception — there's no wiring involved, so there's nothing to assess. Anything that means opening a switch plate is a different matter. You're working on live mains wiring inside a wall, and a smart switch adds a layer standard wiring never had to deal with: whether there's a neutral to power it, whether your specific bulbs are actually compatible with a no-neutral workaround, and — on stairs, landings, or anywhere with two-way or three-way switching — wiring that gets diagram-complicated fast even before "smart" enters the picture. Get it wrong and you're not just left with a switch that flickers or won't connect; you're left with a fault behind a wall. We assess what your existing wiring can actually support, tell you honestly whether a no-neutral switch will behave itself or whether it's worth running a proper neutral feed instead, and fit it properly the first time — so it's the bulbs and the app you're troubleshooting later, never the wiring.
